Supply Chain Outlook: What's on the Horizon in 2022?
January 15, 2022 | by John Fialka, E&E News reporter
What will happen to the global supply chain this year? Will it improve, as President Biden and his team promise? Or will it remain mired in problems caused by pandemic disruptions and geopolitical conflicts, as some analysts predict?
The answers depend on whom you ask. There is widespread agreement that the situation is chaotic and unpredictable. But many experts see cause for hope.
"I am very optimistic about what's going to happen in 2022," said Daniel Ujczo, founder of Supply Chain Matters. "This will be a year where we'll start to see some significant improvements."
Supply chain problems are now top of mind for most Americans as they feel the effects of shortages and price increases at the grocery store, on e-commerce sites like Amazon and in the auto industry. But Biden's administration is putting new emphasis on supply chains after years when it focused more narrowly on trade policy. And companies that have suffered from problems are looking to do more than just survive — they want to prosper by building a competitive advantage over rivals.
"This is no longer a 'supply chain' problem, this is now a matter of national security," said Ujczo, whose Washington-based consulting firm helps businesses make sense out of the complex global supply chains that produce everything from cellphones and cars to medical devices and medicines. "I've been in this field for 35 years, and I have never seen anything like it."
The Biden administration has made a point of visiting factories and ports across the country to see firsthand what's causing disruptions and to announce plans for how to fix them. The White House also has held briefings on supply chains at which industry executives and government officials talk about problems they face in getting products made, shipped and delivered.
One problem is that the pandemic disrupted production lines, shut down ports and forced companies to close their facilities or cut back operations because of labor shortages. To make matters worse, a number of key global trade routes are congested and overburdened as Chinese factories seek to increase output in anticipation of an economic recovery in China later this year.
